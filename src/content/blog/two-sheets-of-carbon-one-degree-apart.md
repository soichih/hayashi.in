Graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms in a hexagonal grid. It conducts electricity well. It is not a superconductor.

Take two sheets of it and stack them, one rotated 1.1 degrees relative to the other, and it becomes one. Below about 1.7 kelvin, resistance drops to zero.

Nothing was added. No dopant, no alloy, no new element. Both layers are the same material they were lying flat on a bench, and neither one does this alone. The only thing that changed is the angle between them.

## What the angle does

When you overlay two identical grids at a small angle, the places where atoms line up drift in and out of registration across the sheet. That interference makes a pattern much larger than either grid, the same effect you see when two window screens overlap. It is called a moiré pattern, and here it is not a visual artifact. It is a real superlattice the electrons live in.

At 1.1 degrees, the moiré cell is about 13 nanometers across, roughly fifty times the spacing between neighboring carbon atoms. Widen the twist to 2 degrees and the cell shrinks by nearly half. The alignment sets the scale of the world the electrons see.

Something specific happens at 1.1 degrees. The electronic bands go flat, which in practice means electrons stop being able to move freely. When their kinetic energy is suppressed like that, the thing that governs their behavior is no longer how fast each one travels. It is how they interact with each other. Superconductivity is one of the things that falls out of that regime. Change the carrier density in the same device and you get the opposite instead: a correlated insulator, a material that refuses to conduct at all.

Same two sheets. Superconductor or insulator depending on how you tune it.

Bistritzer and MacDonald predicted this angle theoretically in 2011. Pablo Jarillo-Herrero's group at MIT found it in the lab in 2018. The field it opened is called twistronics, and it has since spread well beyond carbon. Twisted bilayer molybdenum ditelluride hosts fractional quantum anomalous Hall states, a phase people had been looking for in other systems for decades.

## Why I keep thinking about it

I know what this is going to sound like, so let me be clear that what follows is a metaphor and not a result. Nothing in the physics says anything about people. But it reorganized how I think about a question I had considered settled.

I used to treat capability as the thing that mattered. What are you good at, what do you know, what can you do. That framing sits underneath most of how we hire, most of how we teach, and most of how I have privately evaluated myself for years.

The graphene result points at something the framing misses. Whatever the superconductivity is, it is not a property of either sheet. You cannot find it by studying one of them more carefully. It does not live in the parts at all. It lives in the relationship between them, and it exists only at a particular alignment.

That is closer to how good collaborations actually feel than anything in a skills matrix. Two people who each work fine alone will sometimes produce something neither could have produced, and it is not because either of them leveled up. The same two people, differently aligned, produce friction and nothing else. We usually explain that with vague words like chemistry or fit, as though it were unknowable. Here is a physical system where it is exactly knowable, and the answer is a number: 1.1 degrees.

Two details keep the metaphor from being comfortable.

The first is the precision. It is 1.1 degrees, not around one degree. Experimentalists work hard to control the twist to within about a tenth of a degree, and real devices have enough local variation in the angle that only parts of a sample superconduct while other parts do not. Good alignment is not a mood. It is narrow, it drifts, and it takes deliberate work to hold.

The second is that the flat-band condition works by suppressing individual motion. What makes the interaction dominant is that the electrons stop moving independently so easily. There is a real cost to being in that state, and I do not think that part of the analogy is wrong either. The arrangements where something emerges between people are usually arrangements where each person gives up some of their own free movement.

So the reframing I am left with is not that capability does not matter. It is that capability is the smaller half of the question, and the half I have overweighted. What you can do sets a ceiling. How you are aligned with the people around you determines how much of the space under that ceiling is reachable at all, and it is a much more sensitive parameter than I had assumed.

One degree, in the right direction, changed what carbon is capable of.

## References

- Cao, Fatemi, Fang, Watanabe, Taniguchi, Kaxiras & Jarillo-Herrero, [Unconventional superconductivity in magic-angle graphene superlattices](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.02342) (*Nature*, 2018) - superconductivity up to 1.7 K at a twist angle of 1.1 degrees, at record-low carrier density for a 2D system.
- Bistritzer & MacDonald, [Moiré bands in twisted double-layer graphene](https://arxiv.org/abs/1009.4203) (*PNAS* 108, 12233, 2011) - the theoretical prediction of magic angles, the largest near 1.1 degrees, where the Fermi velocity vanishes and the bands go flat.
- Carr, Massatt, Fang, Cazeaux, Luskin & Kaxiras, [Twistronics: manipulating the electronic properties of two-dimensional layered structures through their twist angle](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.00649) (*Physical Review B* 95, 075420, 2017) - where the name comes from.
- Uri, Grover, Cao et al., [Mapping the twist-angle disorder and Landau levels in magic-angle graphene](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-2255-3) (*Nature*, 2020) - how much the angle varies within a single real device, and what that does to the correlated states.
- Cai, Anderson, Wang et al., [Signatures of fractional quantum anomalous Hall states in twisted MoTe2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06289-w) (*Nature*, 2023) - the same trick, different material, a phase people had hunted for decades.
