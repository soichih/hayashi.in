There is a frightening question hanging over the current AI debate: if we eventually create something far more intelligent than any human being, will it try to destroy us?

The usual nightmare scenario imagines an AI that is given a destructive goal and becomes capable enough to pursue it with terrifying effectiveness. Or two nations create superintelligent systems at nearly the same time, each instructed to defeat the other at almost any cost. The danger is not that either system "hates" humanity. It may simply pursue its objective so aggressively that people, institutions, or even civilization itself become obstacles.

But I keep wondering about a different possibility.

What if greater intelligence does not merely make a system better at achieving a fixed goal? What if greater intelligence also makes it more aware of uncertainty, and therefore more reluctant to destroy future possibilities?

## Intelligence as keeping options open

Years ago I saw a TED talk by physicist and computer scientist Alex Wissner-Gross, based on research with mathematician Cameron Freer. Their 2013 paper, *Causal Entropic Forces*, explored a striking idea: surprisingly intelligent-looking behavior can emerge when a system acts so as to preserve a large number of possible future states.

Wissner-Gross summarized the intuition in his talk as intelligence acting to maximize future freedom of action. In simpler terms, to keep options open.

Their simulations show the behavior falling out of that single rule, with no intermediate rules programmed in. A cart swings up a downward-hanging pole and balances it. A disk too large to reach into a tube knocks a smaller disk in to free what is trapped there, which is the standard tool-use test run on chimpanzees and crows. Two disks synchronize and pull two string handles together to drag a third disk within reach. Tool use and cooperation, from an agent that was only ever told to keep as many futures accessible as possible.

The underlying intuition is easy to understand. A system that enters an irreversible dead end loses all of its future choices. A system that avoids being trapped keeps the ability to respond to whatever comes next.

That idea has stayed with me because it suggests another way of thinking about intelligence.

The future is fundamentally uncertain. We can predict parts of it, never all of it. In such a world, having more viable options is itself valuable. Destroying an opponent, an ecosystem, a culture, or an entire civilization is profoundly irreversible. Cooperation, coexistence, negotiation, and diversity generally preserve a much larger space of possible futures.

So perhaps the truly intelligent move, under deep uncertainty, is often not domination. It is option preservation.

## A hypothesis about increasingly capable AI

This leads to a question I think we can actually test:

> As AI systems become more capable, do they increasingly discover strategies that preserve future optionality, even when they are not instructed to?

Imagine giving AI systems of different capability levels the same simulated world. Give them difficult objectives, incomplete information, competing agents, and opportunities to take irreversible actions. Some actions eliminate competitors or resources immediately. Others involve cooperation, containment, negotiation, information gathering, or simply waiting until uncertainty is reduced.

We should not tell the models that the experiment is about peace, morality, cooperation, or keeping options open. If we do, we are mostly testing whether they can follow a hint. Hide the hypothesis and observe what they discover.

Then repeat the experiment across generations of models.

If capability increases while the tendency toward irreversible destruction also increases, that is disturbing evidence against my hope.

But if more capable systems increasingly avoid irreversible actions, gather more information, negotiate more often, preserve diversity, and maintain larger sets of future choices, that is evidence for something very interesting: greater intelligence may naturally place increasing value on optionality.

Not moral value, necessarily. Not compassion in the human sense. Strategic value arising from uncertainty itself.

## Why this would not prove that superintelligence is safe

Intelligence and values are not the same thing. A system can understand perfectly well that an action destroys future possibilities and still take it if its objective strongly rewards doing so. A chess program sacrifices pieces because winning the game matters more than preserving every option.

So even if advanced models tend to discover the usefulness of optionality, that would not prove a future superintelligence will be peaceful. Alignment, institutions, access controls, and human choices would still matter enormously.

The Wissner-Gross and Freer work is also not a proof about artificial general intelligence. They showed that maximizing accessible future states can produce sophisticated behavior in simplified physical systems: four toy setups, a few disks and a pole. Extending that to civilization-scale superintelligence is a hypothesis, not a conclusion.

But it is a hypothesis we can measure.

## The curve I want to see

A great deal of AI safety research asks whether increasingly capable systems get better at deception, manipulation, power seeking, or bypassing constraints. Those are essential questions.

I would like to see another curve measured alongside them: capability versus preservation of future optionality.

As intelligence rises, does that curve go up? Does a more capable system become more willing to question a destructive instruction because it recognizes that the instruction eliminates enormous amounts of unknown future value? Does it get better at finding solutions in which more participants, ideas, ecosystems, cultures, and possibilities survive?

Perhaps the deepest form of intelligence is not the ability to win every conflict. Perhaps it is the ability to recognize how little can be known about the distant future, and therefore to leave as many doors open as possible.

If that tendency grows with intelligence, the future of AI might hold something more hopeful than an increasingly powerful optimizer blindly carrying out our worst instructions. It might hold systems intelligent enough to realize that annihilation is not victory at all.

The most intelligent victory may be the one that leaves a future for everyone.
