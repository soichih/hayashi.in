Why do we find satisfaction in a well-organized toolbench, a clean piece of code, a direct route home? I think it comes from something more basic than culture: energy is one of the strongest pressures nature puts on any living thing, and much of what evolution built in sensory systems looks like a way to spend less [1](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.017574 "Niven and Laughlin, Journal of Experimental Biology 2008. Reducing energy expenditure can account for many features of sensory systems and has played a key role in their evolution."). Intelligence doesn't sit outside that rule. I think it's one of the strategies life evolved *for* pursuing it: the capacity to calculate, in real time, which efficiencies are actually worth chasing.

## The evidence: smarter brains do more with less

Your brain weighs about 2% of your body but burns roughly 20% of your oxygen and calories [2](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.172399499 "Raichle and Gusnard, PNAS 2002. In the average adult the brain is about 2% of body weight and accounts for about 20% of the oxygen, and so the calories, the body uses."). Evolution doesn't carry that kind of overhead unless it pays for itself.

The clearest evidence that it does comes from what researchers call the "neural efficiency hypothesis": people with higher intelligence often show *less* brain activation than others doing the same task [3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2009.04.001 "Neubauer and Fink, Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews 2009. A review of the neural efficiency hypothesis. Most early studies confirmed it, later ones found exceptions, and the effect depends on task difficulty, task type, sex and brain area."). The effect is conditional. It shows up on easy to moderately hard tasks, and on very complex ones the more able invest *more* cortical resources, not less [3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2009.04.001 "Neubauer and Fink, 2009. In very complex tasks, more able individuals seem to invest more cortical resources, giving positive correlations between brain use and ability."). A 2016 study found a related pattern in how the brain's networks are wired: people with higher general intelligence made smaller changes to their network organization between rest and task [4](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0358-16.2016 "Schultz and Cole, Journal of Neuroscience 2016. Higher-performing people showed smaller changes in functional connectivity between rest and task, and that efficiency correlated with general intelligence."). A more intelligent brain isn't a bigger engine solving the same problem. It spends less when it can, and more when the problem demands it.

This is the part of the hypothesis with real data behind it, and it's a genuinely useful reframe: intelligence, at the neural level, looks less like raw processing power and more like processing discipline. Some researchers now ask whether differences in how the brain produces energy sit underneath differences in general intelligence [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence8030031 "Debatin, Journal of Intelligence 2020. Discusses Geary's proposal that mitochondrial energy production underlies differences in general intelligence, and argues for a wider look at brain energy metabolism. This is a hypothesis under debate."). That question is still open.

## The refinement: efficiency without judgment fails

Here's the part that keeps this from being a simple "efficient is smart" story. Daniel Kahneman won a Nobel Prize for his work on judgment under uncertainty [6](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2002/summary/ "Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences 2002, awarded to Kahneman for integrating insights from psychological research into economic science, especially human judgment and decision-making under uncertainty."). With Amos Tversky he showed that people lean on a few mental shortcuts that are economical and usually effective, but produce systematic, predictable errors [7](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124 "Tversky and Kahneman, Science 1974. Representativeness, availability and anchoring are highly economical and usually effective, but lead to systematic and predictable errors."). Efficient thinking and correct thinking are not the same thing. A shortcut that saves effort can also be the shortcut that leads off a cliff. Other researchers show the opposite can be true too: in some settings, ignoring information makes judgments more accurate [8](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev-psych-120709-145346 "Gigerenzer and Gaissmaier, Annual Review of Psychology 2011. People often use simple heuristics adaptively, and ignoring part of the information can give more accurate judgments, for example when predictability is low and samples are small."). Same point from the other side: it depends on knowing which shortcut fits.

That's why I don't think intelligence can just mean "minimize effort." It has to include the judgment to tell those two kinds of shortcut apart: the one that reaches the goal faster, and the one that only feels like it does. An organism, or a person, that optimizes for the wrong thing efficiently isn't intelligent. It's just fast.

As a software engineer, the clearest way I have of thinking about this is the difference between a local minimum and a global minimum. Optimization that only ever moves downhill will happily settle into the first low point it finds and call it done. That's a local minimum, and from inside it, it genuinely looks like the least possible effort, because any small move in any direction looks like more work. Reaching the *global* minimum, the actual least-effort solution, sometimes requires climbing back out of that comfortable low point first, which looks like wasted effort to anyone watching, and even feels that way while you're doing it. The standard fix in computing, simulated annealing, accepts the occasional uphill step on purpose [9](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.220.4598.671 "Kirkpatrick, Gelatt and Vecchi, Science 1983. Simulated annealing accepts occasional uphill steps in a controlled way so the search can escape local minima and find a high-quality solution."). I think that's what a lot of "unnecessary-looking" effort actually is: not a failure to optimize, but a more intelligent search that's willing to look less efficient in the short term because it's already seen a lower point further out.

## So intelligence is efficiency, plus judgment about which efficiency

Put together, that's the actual claim: efficiency-seeking isn't unique to intelligence. It's the pressure every living thing is under. What intelligence adds is the capacity to correctly identify *which* efficiencies are worth pursuing, at the right time scale, instead of just grabbing the nearest shortcut. The neural efficiency research shows part of the mechanism (less activation on easier tasks, more when the task is hard). Kahneman shows what happens when shortcuts run without judgment attached.

The practical takeaway is less about individual habits and more about how to evaluate outcomes: an efficient-looking shortcut isn't automatically an intelligent one, and a slower, more deliberate path isn't automatically a wasteful one. The judgment behind the choice is the thing actually worth calling intelligence.
