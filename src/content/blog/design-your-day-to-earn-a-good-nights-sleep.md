I used to think of sleep as maintenance — you refuel at night so you can work the next day. Lately I've started to wonder if it's the other way around.

What if the day is the means, and the night is the end? We work, eat, resolve our small conflicts, tie off our loose threads — not to prepare for tomorrow's labor, but to earn a night of undisturbed sleep. The day is in service of the rest, not the other way around.

There's a version of this in the old idea of dying at peace: that a life well spent is one you can set down without regret. Scale that down to a single day, and the question changes shape [1](https://garenhart.com/judgment/seneca-daily-judgment-seat/ "Seneca, On Anger III, on Sextius reviewing the day before sleep. Seneca asks how sweet the sleep is that follows this self-examination.") [2](https://psyche.co/guides/reflections-on-mortality-can-help-you-live-well-now-heres-how "Joanna Ebenstein, Psyche. On memento mori, the long human practice of keeping a relationship with death as part of living well."). Not "did I get everything done," but "can I close my eyes without an unresolved argument, an unspoken apology, an unfinished worry, tugging at me [3](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000374 "Scullin et al., Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 2018. In 57 healthy young adults, writing a to-do list for 5 minutes at bedtime led to falling asleep significantly faster than writing about completed tasks. The more specific the list, the faster they fell asleep.") [4](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15402010bsm0401_2 "Carney and Waters, Behavioral Sleep Medicine 2006. In 33 students with insomnia, recording possible solutions to worries lowered pre-sleep cognitive arousal compared with just recording worries.") [5](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w "Ghibellini and Meier, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications 2025. A meta-analysis found a general tendency to resume interrupted tasks. It found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks, which the older Zeigarnik effect had claimed.")?"

Maybe that's the real measure of a day — not its productivity, but its ability to let you rest. Each day, in this frame, becomes a small rehearsal for the ending we all eventually write: did I leave things a little better than I found them, so I can sleep, and so whoever comes after can sleep a little easier too.

## References

1. Seneca, on Sextius's nightly practice, in [*On Anger* III](https://garenhart.com/judgment/seneca-daily-judgment-seat/) - the Stoic habit of asking each night, before sleep, "What bad habit of yours have you cured to-day? what vice have you checked? in what respect are you better?"
2. Joanna Ebenstein, ["Reflections on mortality can help you live well now"](https://psyche.co/guides/reflections-on-mortality-can-help-you-live-well-now-heres-how) (*Psyche*) - on memento mori as a practice, the same scaling-down of a whole-life question onto a single day that this post borrows for sleep.
3. Michael K. Scullin et al., ["The effects of bedtime writing on difficulty falling asleep: a polysomnographic study comparing to-do lists and completed activity lists"](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000374) (*Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 147(1), 2018) - 57 young adults fell asleep faster after writing a to-do list than after listing completed tasks. [Baylor University summary](https://news.web.baylor.edu/news/story/2018/can-writing-your-dos-help-you-doze-baylor-study-suggests-jotting-down-tasks-can).
4. Carney & Waters, ["Effects of a structured problem-solving procedure on pre-sleep cognitive arousal in college students with insomnia"](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15402010bsm0401_2) (*Behavioral Sleep Medicine* 4(1), 2006) - students who wrote down possible solutions to their worries had less pre-sleep cognitive arousal than those who only recorded worries.
5. Ghibellini & Meier, ["Interruption, recall and resumption: a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects"](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w) (*Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, 2025) - finds a general tendency to resume interrupted tasks (the Ovsiankina effect) but no memory advantage for unfinished ones, so the popular "unfinished tasks are remembered better" version of the Zeigarnik effect did not hold up.
