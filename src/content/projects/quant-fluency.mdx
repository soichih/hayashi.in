- **Live:** [hayashi.in/quant](/quant/)

I wanted to learn quantitative asset management the way people learn a language: a few
minutes a day, lots of repetition, and each idea seen from several sides until it sticks.
Flashcards didn't fit, because recognizing an answer isn't the same as being able to explain
it. So in Quant Fluency you write your answer in a sentence or two, and a model grades how well
you understood the idea.

The course map has 227 concepts, from what a bond is up to Black-Litterman, risk parity and
backtest overfitting. I built it from the topics covered by the CFA, FRM, CQF, CAIA and CIPM
programs (the site isn't affiliated with any of them) and arranged it in eight tiers with
prerequisites, so a concept unlocks once you've seen the ones it builds on. The first tier is
written: 21 concepts, each with a lesson, a longer "More detail" section with worked examples
and links to primary sources such as the SEC, FINRA and CFA Institute, and three questions that
come at it from different angles: explain it, apply it, calculate it, spot the misconception.

## How it works

- You answer in writing. There's no multiple choice. A **qwen3.5 9B** model running on my home
  server through Ollama compares your answer with a reference answer and a list of key points,
  scores it from 0 to 100, and tells you what you left out. It grades meaning, not wording.
- Two numbers sit at the top of the page. **Mastered** is the share of concepts you've been
  shown that you now know. **Covered** is the share of the whole course you've been shown at
  all. The first says how well you know what you've seen. The second says how much you
  haven't seen yet.
- Passing a concept schedules it to come back after 1, 3, 7, 21 and then 60 days, each time as
  a different question. A concept counts as mastered after three passes spread over at least
  four days, on at least two different questions, so nothing is mastered on the first day.
- After each answer you can review the lesson or ask a tutor follow-up questions, up to five
  per card. Diagrams, in the lessons and in the tutor's answers, are drawn with Mermaid.
- Progress lives in your browser. There are no accounts, and answers aren't stored on the
  server.

## What I ran into

**A small model grades well but explains badly.** Grading against a reference answer is a
narrow job, and the 9B model does it well in about two seconds: in my tests it gave zero to
wrong answers and to answers that tried to talk it into a perfect score. Explaining is harder.
When I ran 27 realistic follow-up questions through the same model, more than a third of its
explanations had a real error, like a backwards bid and ask or an arithmetic slip, and the
charts it drew were often wrong. A stricter prompt helped but didn't fix it, and turning on its
reasoning mode made things worse: it spent its whole budget thinking and returned nothing. So
grading stays on the home server, and the tutor uses a larger model through Ollama's cloud
service, which got every number right when I reran the questions the small model missed.
That means your follow-up questions leave my server, while your graded answers don't.

**The browser never sends the right answer.** It would be simpler for the page to send your
answer and the reference answer together, but then the endpoint would grade anything anyone
sent it, which makes it a free chatbot on my GPU. The page sends only a question id and your
text, and the server looks up the reference itself. The tutor works the same way, with a limit
on questions per card and an instruction to stay on the course's topics.

**Charts need to survive a phone screen.** Mermaid only draws a chart legend when the chart is
about 600 pixels wide, so on a phone a two-line chart showed two colors and no labels. The page
now draws charts at the width they're shown and adds its own legend underneath.

The next tier covers the statistics underneath everything else: variance, correlation,
regression, and the distributions that show up in risk models.
